1. Simplicity critique

My biggest pushback: you are trying to make the RSVP form do too much.

The RSVP is not the lead-capture moment. The RSVP is the trust moment. Your job is to get the parent through it quickly, then earn the right to ask for marketing permission.

Partiful is a useful reference here: its RSVP starts with the response and then asks for basic identity/verification; its questionnaire is a separate configurable layer.  Paperless Post similarly treats guest questions as an event-management feature rather than the invitation itself. 

I would change your fields

Required:

* Attending: Yes / No / Maybe
* Parent name
* Child first name
* Parent mobile only if needed for event communication
* Adults staying: 0 / 1 / 2+

Conditional:

* Food allergy? Yes / No
    * If yes → “Please tell us what we should know”
* Additional child → child first name

Optional:

* Email
* Child birthday month

Marketing: NOT part of the core RSVP.

I would actually make parent mobile optional unless the guest explicitly chooses SMS communication. If someone says “No marketing” but you’re requiring their mobile anyway, you’ve created unnecessary privacy friction.

The exact legal language should be reviewed for your actual SMS program/terms, but conceptually this is the right separation.

Don’t ask for email + SMS + birthday + offers + social follow + referral all on the RSVP screen.

That is how you turn a magical invitation into a lead-gen form.

You already know all of it.

3. Lead capture mechanics

Here’s my ranking.

Rank	Tactic	Expected impact
1	Post-RSVP $100 birthday offer	Very high
2	Birthday-month capture	Very high
3	Free premium add-on	High
4	Dedicated Birthday Club	High
5	Post-party personalized offer	High
6	QR code at party	Medium-high
7	Goody-bag card	Medium
8	Shared photo album	Medium
9	Host referral credit	Medium-high
10	SMS opt-in	Medium initially, high long-term

#1 — $100 first-party offer

I’d test:

Want a birthday party like this one?

Get $100 off your child’s first Host Hampton party.

☐ Yes! Send me my birthday perk.

This is much stronger than:

“Subscribe to our newsletter.”

Nobody wants a newsletter.

They may want $100 toward their child’s birthday.

I’d make it valid for 12 months, not 30 days. You’re dealing with children’s birthdays. Artificial urgency will hurt trust.

#2 — Birthday month

Ask after RSVP:

When’s your child’s birthday?
January · February · March…

I would not require the date.

Month is enough to trigger the marketing engine.

Then:

We’ll send you a birthday reminder and your Host Hampton birthday perk when your month gets close. 💕

That is genuinely useful rather than creepy.

#3 — Free add-on

I’d test this against $100 off.

For example:

Your Host Hampton birthday perk

Book your first party and choose a complimentary Glow Upgrade, Hair Tinsel, or Glitter Tattoo Station.

Why I like this:

A $100 discount teaches the customer that Host Hampton can be negotiated.

A $100-ish value-add reinforces that Host Hampton is premium.

This is particularly important for your affluent Hamptons segment.

I’d A/B test:

A: $100 off
B: complimentary premium add-on

My initial bet for Host Hampton: B wins on brand; A wins on raw conversion.

#4 — Birthday Club

Don’t call it a newsletter.

Call it:

Host Hampton Birthday Club

Position it as:

Birthday inspiration, new themes and a special birthday perk each year.

That feels like membership rather than marketing.

#5 — Ask after RSVP, not before

This is probably the most important funnel decision.

RSVP → confirmation → optional offer

not:

RSVP → marketing checkbox → continue

The first is:

“Thanks for coming!”

The second feels like:

“Give us your data before we’ll let you RSVP.”

Exact wording I’d test

Email:

☐ Yes! I’d love Host Hampton birthday perks by email.
Send me my $100 birthday credit, new theme ideas and occasional party specials.

SMS:

☐ Yes, send me occasional Host Hampton party updates by text.

Then separately explain the applicable SMS terms/frequency and opt-out mechanism in your consent disclosure.

The FCC has emphasized that consumers must have a reasonable way to revoke consent from texting, and opt-out handling needs to be operationally reliable.

This is where I think you’re leaving a lot on the table.

You have something Facebook ads can never buy:

And unlike an Instagram visitor, they’ve just watched their child have an amazing time.

At check-in

Don’t put a giant marketing QR code on the front desk.

Instead:

with a small QR:

During the party

Take great photos.

But here’s the important part:

The photo experience is the lead magnet.

Afterward:

View + download the photos from Cora’s party.

Then:

Planning a birthday of your own?

Get your $100 birthday perk

That’s a much more natural transition than:

CTA:

Plan the party

Rule I’d impose

Maximum 1 marketing email/month outside the birthday window.

And probably zero SMS marketing initially unless someone explicitly opts into it.

SMS should be your high-value channel for:

* reservation reminders
* important event updates
* opted-in promotions

Not:

“HEY!!! NEW THEME!!!”

That will burn your list.

CAN-SPAM requires a clear mechanism for recipients to opt out of marketing email.

6. Referral loop

Yes.

And I would tell the host parent upfront—but subtly.

I’d make the host confirmation say:

One more thing…

If one of your party guests ends up celebrating with us too, we’ll send $100 Host Hampton credit your way.

No referral codes needed—we’ll automatically connect the booking to your party.

That’s excellent psychologically.

It turns the host into your salesperson without asking them to sell.

But don’t make the invitation say:

“Refer your friends and earn $100!”

That’s tacky.

The invitation belongs to the birthday child.

The referral mechanism belongs in the host dashboard/confirmation.

Track it automatically

Each party gets:

party_id

Each guest RSVP gets:

guest_id

Every guest receives a unique:

referral_token

If that parent eventually books:

booking.referral_source = guest_id

Then:

Guest books → host gets $100 credit

I’d cap it at perhaps $300 per original party initially.

And don’t pay cash.

Host Hampton credit is better.

8. Risks

1. You’re collecting more child data than you need

I’d eliminate:

* full child name
* exact DOB
* child email
* child phone
* address
* unnecessary profile information

First name + perhaps birthday month is plenty.

COPPA treats children’s names, online contact information and other identifiers as personal information, and special obligations can apply when information is collected directly from children. 

Your system should therefore make it extremely clear:

This RSVP is completed by a parent/guardian.

Don’t design the experience as though children are the users.

⸻

2. Allergies

This is more sensitive than a normal RSVP field.

Don’t use:

“Tell us anything about your child.”

Use:

Food allergies or dietary concerns?

Then collect only what’s operationally necessary.

Don’t let allergies become part of a marketing profile.

⸻

3. Guest list exposure

Don’t automatically show:

Sarah + Emma + Emma’s mom + phone number…

to everyone.

The host can see the guest list.

Guests should probably see only:

12 friends are coming 🎉

or nothing at all.

Partiful specifically provides controls for anonymizing guest lists and hiding guest counts, which is a good signal that this is a real privacy consideration in event products. 

⸻

4. “You gave us your phone number, therefore marketing”

Absolutely not.

Your architecture should have distinct consent states:

transactional_event_sms
marketing_email
marketing_sms

Not:

marketing_consent = true

And store:

* timestamp
* consent language/version
* source/party
* channel
* IP/user-agent if appropriate
* opt-out timestamp

⸻

5. Data security

You’re accumulating a very attractive local-parent database:

name + phone + email + child name + birthday month + allergy information.

That needs to be treated seriously.

New York’s SHIELD Act requires businesses maintaining covered private information to maintain reasonable administrative, technical and physical safeguards. 

At minimum:

* Supabase RLS
* no guest-to-guest data leakage
* admin MFA
* separate production credentials
* encrypted transport
* minimal retention
* audit logging for admin access
* don’t expose raw Supabase IDs in public URLs
* signed/unguessable invitation tokens

And I’d seriously consider deleting old allergy data after the event/retention period rather than keeping it forever.

⸻

6. The biggest brand risk

Making Mom feel like she accidentally enrolled her friends into Host Hampton marketing.

This could kill the product.

The golden rule:

The birthday invitation is never an advertisement.

The marketing happens after the guest has received what they came for.

9. Metrics

Don’t drown yourself in analytics.

Track these five.

1. Invitation → RSVP conversion

Target [ESTIMATE]: 70–90%

Measure:

unique invitation visitors → completed RSVP

This is your core UX metric.

⸻

2. RSVP completion rate

Target [ESTIMATE]: >85%

Once someone starts the RSVP:

started RSVP → completed RSVP

If this is <80%, your form is too long or confusing.

⸻

3. Marketing opt-in rate

This is your money metric.

Track separately:

* email opt-in
* SMS opt-in
* either-channel opt-in

Initial target [ESTIMATE]: 25–40%

I would be thrilled with 40%+ from a genuinely optional post-RSVP offer.

Don’t optimize toward 80% by making the UX coercive.

⸻

4. Guest → booking conversion

The ultimate metric:

opted-in guest families → Host Hampton bookings

Target [ESTIMATE]: 3–10% over 12 months.

Don’t judge this after 30 days. Kids’ birthdays are annual and seasonal.

⸻

5. Revenue per party guest

This is the metric I’d obsess over.

Example:

10 guests
× 35% opt-in
× 5% booking
= 0.175 bookings per party

At a hypothetical $900 average booking:

$157.50 incremental expected booking revenue per original party

That’s the actual economics of this system.

And that’s why I would absolutely build it.

My strongest recommendation

I’d actually rename the product internally from “e-vite system” to something like:

Host Hampton Party Pass

Because you’re not really building an invitation tool.

You’re building a closed-loop customer acquisition system disguised as a fantastic invitation experience.

The flywheel becomes:

Booking
↓
Beautiful personalized invite
↓
Host shares in one tap
↓
Guest RSVPs in <30 sec
↓
Guest has a delightful experience
↓
Optional Birthday Club
↓
Birthday month captured
↓
Personalized nurture
↓
Guest books
↓
Original host receives Host Hampton credit
↓
New party creates another 10–20 prospects

That’s much more powerful than simply replacing Canva.

And there’s an important strategic insight here: don’t try to maximize the number of contacts you collect. Maximize the number of parents who voluntarily identify themselves as “I might want a Host Hampton party someday.”

Those are fundamentally different goals.

If you make the RSVP experience feel like a lead form, you’ll collect fewer good leads—and potentially damage the social trust that makes the whole distribution mechanism work in the first place.

The existing invitation products validate the basic interaction model—shareable link, mobile RSVP, guest questions, real-time guest management and photo sharing—but Host Hampton has an advantage they don’t: every event is already a qualified, geographically relevant customer-acquisition opportunity. 

That’s the moat I’d build around.
