## What this form does — the short version

Please read the whole thing, but here is the summary:

1. We tell you honestly what the activities involve, so you can decide what your child takes part in.
2. You tell us about allergies, medical conditions and who to call in an emergency.
3. You choose — genuinely yes or no — whether we may photograph your child and where we may use those photos.

5. You confirm the booking, payment and cancellation terms you already saw when you booked.

**This form does not ask you to give up your right to hold us responsible if we are careless.** We are not asking for that, and under New York law we could not enforce it anyway.

## 4. What the activities actually involve

We would rather tell you plainly than bury it. Depending on the package you booked, the party may include some of:

- **Canvas, splatter and glow painting** — paints and pigments that **stain skin and clothing**. Splatter painting is messy by design. Dress accordingly; we are not responsible for stained clothes.
- **Sand art, seashell decorating, squishies** — **small parts**, which are a choking hazard for younger children and for any child who still mouths objects.
- **Slime** — ingredients typically include glue, borate-based activators and colourants. **Not for eating.** Some children get skin irritation.
- **Drip-paint balloon dogs** — balloons. **Uninflated and burst balloon pieces are a serious choking hazard** for young children, and some children have latex allergies.
- **Bracelet and jewelry making** — beads, small findings, cords, sometimes scissors or pliers.

- **Glitter tattoos and hair tinsel** — cosmetic-grade glitter, skin adhesive, and tools used close to the face and scalp.
- **Kids' spa** — kid-safe nail polish and gentle face masks applied to skin.

- **Permanent jewelry** — see Section 5. This one is different and has its own acknowledgment.
- **Active play, moving around, and food** brought by you or your caterer.

**Risks that come with all of this** include: allergic or skin reactions to paints, adhesives, glitter, latex, cosmetics, metals or food; choking on small parts or balloon pieces; eye irritation; cuts or pinches from craft tools; burns from any heat tool used; slips, trips and falls; and permanent staining of clothing and belongings.

**By signing, you confirm you have read this list, you understand these risks are part of the activity, and you are choosing to let your child take part.** You can tell us at any point that your child should sit out an activity, and we will make that easy.

## 5. Permanent jewelry — separate acknowledgment

Permanent jewelry is fitted by making a **small micro-weld on a chain while it is on the body**. It is a distinct physical process and deserves its own paragraph.

If you or your child are having permanent jewelry fitted, you confirm that you understand:

- The weld produces a **brief spark and flash**. Protective eyewear is provided and **must be worn by the person being fitted and by anyone watching closely.** Children crowd in — please help us keep them back.
- There is a small risk of **momentary heat, minor burns or skin irritation** at the weld point.
- The chain is metal. **If you have any metal sensitivity or allergy — nickel in particular — tell us before we begin.** Note the metal used in Section 12 notes.
- **Do not proceed if you have a pacemaker or another implanted electronic medical device**, unless your doctor has cleared it.
- **"Permanent" means no clasp — not unremovable, and not permanent-safe.** It is removed by cutting the chain, which you can do at home with scissors or wire cutters. **It is not MRI-safe and is not a medical alert device.** Remove it before an MRI, surgery, or any medical imaging, and before contact sports.
- The chain can **snag, break or wear** with normal use. Ask us about re-welding.
- **A cut chain is a final sale.**

**For anyone under 18, a parent or legal guardian must be physically present at the fitting and must sign.** We will not fit permanent jewelry on a guest child on the host's say-so.

## 11. Damage, and the $500 hold

**This is ordinary contract, not a waiver — and it works.**

You are responsible for damage to our studio, our equipment or our materials caused by you or your guests, beyond normal use of the space. That includes damage from decor you attach to something, and cleaning beyond what a party normally needs.

**About the $500 hold:**

- We place a **refundable $500 authorization hold** on your card **on arrival**.
- **This is a hold, not a charge.** It reserves $500 on your card. **No money moves to us.** It is not a payment, and it does not appear as a purchase.
- We **release it after your event**. Once we release it, your bank returns the amount on its own schedule.
- **If you pay by debit card, the hold reduces the money actually available in your bank account**, and your bank may take several business days to return it after we release it. On a credit card it only uses part of your credit line. **We recommend a credit card.**
- We only capture any part of it if there is damage or excessive cleaning. **If that happens, we will email you photos and an itemized amount before we capture anything.**
- Anything above $500 remains your responsibility, and we will invoice you for it with the same evidence.

## 13. The legal bits

**New York law.** This agreement is governed by the law of the State of New York.

**Severability and savings.** If any part of this agreement is held invalid or unenforceable, that part is severed and **everything else stays in full force**. Where possible, an unenforceable provision should be read as narrowly as needed to make it enforceable rather than struck entirely. **In particular: if any acknowledgment or limitation in this agreement is unenforceable under New York General Obligations Law § 5-326 or any other law, the remainder — including the risk disclosure in Section 4, the medical authorization in Section 7, the photo consent in Section 8, the supervision rules in Section 6, the damage responsibility in Section 11 and the booking terms in Section 12 — remains fully effective.**

**What this agreement does not do.** Nothing here limits Host Hampton's responsibility for its own negligence, gross negligence, recklessness or intentional acts, and nothing here waives any claim belonging to a child.

**Electronic signature.** You agree that signing electronically has the same effect as signing on paper, under the federal ESIGN Act and New York's Electronic Signatures and Records Act.

**Whole agreement.** This form, together with the booking confirmation and quote you received, is the whole agreement about your party.

> `[VERIFY WITH ATTORNEY]` — this draft deliberately contains **no** liability release, **no** indemnification clause and **no** limitation of liability, on the reasoning that (a) § 5-326 likely voids a release at the studio, (b) *Alexander v. Kendall Central School District*, 221 A.D.2d 898 (1995) means a parent cannot bind the child, and (c) *Valdimer* makes parental indemnity for the child's claim very likely void — and that including unenforceable clauses creates false confidence and reads as overreaching. **If you disagree and want a narrowly drawn release, an indemnity limited to third-party claims caused by the Client's own guests, or a mobile-only release, please draft them.** Note *Gross v. Sweet*, 49 N.Y.2d 102 (1979): any exculpatory language must name negligence in unmistakable terms or it fails regardless.

## Must decide

1. **Does § 5-326 apply to the Speonk studio?** If yes (assume so), confirm no release is worth including. If you think the *Lemoine v. Cornell* / *Scrivener* instructional line is available — Host Hampton sells hosted, taught craft activities, not open admission to a play space — say so, because it would change how the packages should be described in marketing as well as here.
2. **Does § 5-326 apply to mobile parties?** No New York case was found either way. If it does not, a **separate mobile agreement** could carry a real release. Advise whether to split the document. The owner asked for one combined document; this draft complies, and flags the cost.
3. **Release, indemnity, liability cap — in or out?** This draft has none. State a position on each, including whether a narrow indemnity for third-party claims caused by the Client's guests is worth having.
4. **Guest children.** Confirm the per-child-form rule in Section 1 in light of *Gayles v. Sky Zone*, and whether the Client should be contractually obliged to forward the link.
5. **3% credit-card surcharge and GBL § 518.** Review across the whole booking flow, not just this form: total-dollar disclosure before payment, no percentage-only statement, no debit surcharging, surcharge not above actual cost of acceptance. Penalties run to $500 per violation.
6. **Photo consent scope.** Time-limited and withdrawable (parent-friendly) versus the broad unrestricted consent *Shields v. Gross* upheld. Business trade-off, but confirm the narrower version still gets §§ 50–51 protection within its stated scope.
7. **Insurance.** Give this to the insurer as well as the attorney. Confirm the general-liability policy is **occurrence-based, not claims-made** — a child injured at a party in 2026 can sue until roughly 2043 under CPLR 208, long after a claims-made policy would have lapsed. **Nothing in this draft states or implies any insurance coverage, because none was verified.**
8. **Records retention.** Because of the CPLR 208 infancy toll, signed agreements, incident reports, staff rosters and photos should be retained for **20 years or more**. In New York this matters more than the contract language.
